1. The audit fixes (done)

The cold open's freeze, and the hitches when a level opens

The heavy pictures are now painted in a background worker, off the main thread, before they are needed. That covers the opening's six pictures, the Landing's sky, all five planets (cold and relit) and every level's sky. The page paints a picture itself only if the browser has no worker. A second cause turned up while measuring: the Stillness's picture was repainted every time it changed size as it swelled during the loss, which cost a 30–50 ms frame at 29.8 s. It is now painted once at its largest and drawn scaled.

Emberwick's spam bot

The stretch was Ossa's hammer, from the 19.2 s checkpoint to 35.5 s. The chart there asks for about two taps a second. A bot tapping steadily three times a second never counted as mashing, because the rule needed more than twice the chart's rate. With the buffer, one of its taps almost always landed in each wall's window. I added a second half to the rule: two wasted taps within two seconds is also mashing (a wasted tap is one that opens nothing, isn't held for an opening, touches no note and isn't part of a hold). When the rule triggers, the buffer is off and stray taps near an opening hit the ring, as before.

2. Honest assessment: what the engine can do today

Planet hopsThey exist: a sling bar (one tap) or spring bar (no tap) throws Orbin to the next planet in a 2-beat flight. A target marks the landing two beats ahead, and the camera glides with him. None of World 1's five levels uses them yet. Three limits: every flight is exactly 2 beats with nothing to tap in the air; the next planet always sits on a fixed zig-zag; and every planet looks the same, because the level's world paints one planet and that planet follows Orbin.Places: each hop lands on its own painted body (a moon, the twins, a bridge) with its own size and position. The timing model already handles flights; the new parts are taps in the air (simulation and bots) and the places' art (painted in the worker, so no hitches).

What changes is the drawing. Each path becomes a curve with its own centre line and "up" direction: an ellipse, a figure-eight, a spiral, a straight bridge, or a path round an odd-shaped body. Distance along the curve is set by beats, so speed always matches the music.Large. This is the biggest part: the renderer's ring drawing is rebuilt around a general path. Risks

  • Timing: low for shapes, because they only change the drawing. The real risk is taps in the air during flights, which is new simulation code. It gets the same bot tests as everything else: on time, early, from every checkpoint, spam bots, the sloppy player.
  • Readability: the main risk. A figure-eight crosses itself, so only the lane Orbin is on can be lit; the other stays dark stone. A bridge needs the camera ahead of him, not centred on a planet. Each launch has to show its landing first. If any frame shows less than 1.5 s of path ahead, the audit fails it.
  • Motion: more camera moves. The reduce-flash setting will also calm the reframing and the shakes.
  • Performance: places are painted in the worker, so no hitches. Curved paths cost a bit more to draw than arcs. The iPhone is still the unknown, so your ?phonecheck numbers matter before this lands.
  • Scope: Emberwick first, as you said. Applying it to the other four worlds is the same work again, mostly art and charting, since the engine part is done once.

The places, phone-sized

Left to right: the Ash Moon (the bellows ahead, Emberwick dark in the sky) · the fling (the Kindling's ring of sparks, an ember in the air with its gold ring, the landing already marked) · the forge ring (Meteor, Ossa's anvils, one already split behind him) · the Twins (the crossing, the hammer's ring of fire ahead, the next landing marked top left) · the Slag Bridge (straight at Emberwick, a slab to dash, a vent) · the dive (the spiral into the forge's mouth).

Three interactions, three beats each

  • Pad: the bellows. Anticipation: as you come, the bellows breathe in (the leather draws up, ash is pulled toward the nozzle, the iron glows) and the gold ring closes on it. Orbin crouches. Contact: the bellows slam shut, a blast of air and sparks, a flash; for one frame Orbin is squashed flat on the boards. Follow-through: he's high in the air and stretched along the arc, tail streaming, while the bellows wheeze open and puffs of ash drift. He lands with a squash and a ring of embers where the soft target was.
  • Orb: an ember in the air. Anticipation: the coal brightens and its rim turns faster as the beat comes. Contact: he snaps up through it and it bursts into a ring of sparks. Follow-through: the dark husk falls and his sparks trail with him down the far side.
  • Wall: Ossa's anvil. Anticipation: the anvil's face heats white and sparks rise off it, and the Meteor leans in, flattened. Contact: a scale punch (the anvil a size bigger for a frame), a flash, a short shake (visual only; the music never stops). Follow-through: the halves tumble outward, molten drips fall, and his streak runs on beyond.

Moving between objects, Orbin bobs on the beat, leans into curves, streams his tail and sheds sparks. The anticipation never adds a second gold signal: objects warm in their own colours, and the gold stays on the one tap that's next.
