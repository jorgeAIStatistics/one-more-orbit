ONE MORE ORBIT: art bible

Three directions for a mature, hand-made look, each shown as the same four pictures: the hero, the boss, the galaxy map, and a moment of play during a drop. Nothing in the game has changed. Pick one (or a mix) and I apply it everywhere.

What these pictures are, plainly. I drew all twelve in code, on a canvas, with tools I wrote for the job: paper grain and fibres, uneven washes, brush passes, a burin's wavering hatching, gold leaf with flecks, halftone dots, plates out of register. No image generator and no stock art. The upside is that everything you see here can be drawn by the game itself. The limit is real too: code can make texture, light and good shapes, but it is not a painter's hand. Section 5 is the brief for a human illustrator, for the key art where that hand matters most.

1. What changes in every direction

  • No faces. Eko is a flame with a seed of light in it, a wisp that follows a beat behind, and the ring he wears. He speaks through how he leans, flares and trails. The Hush has no mouth and no cute eye: it is a mass that takes light, with one lidded sliver that looks back.
  • Light has a source. On the map every planet is lit from the Lantern, so its lit side always faces the lighthouse. In play, Eko and the planet are the lights. The Hush gives none.
  • Texture on everything, sparkles on nothing. Stars are dabs, points or dots, gathered into bands. The four-point sparkle is gone.
  • One language from map to play. The same paper, the same line or brush, the same type. The planet you chose on the map is the one at the centre of your rings.
  • Play stays clean. Rings, marks, hazards and Eko are drawn last, on a calm ground, with a dark edge under them. White is still only ever "tap here". Style lives behind, in the effects and in the small shapes.
  • Each planet has its own palette, tied to its song (listed per direction below).

My recommendation: B, Star Atlas, with one thing borrowed from A: a single wash of colour per planet under the engraving, and A's swelling light for the drop. It is the most distinctive of the three, it turns the game's own idea (music you read on a ring) into its look, it is the easiest to keep readable, and it is the one I can make feel hand-made without a painter. A is the most moving but depends most on a human hand. C is the boldest but the closest to where we are now.

5. Brief for an illustrator (draft, one page)

Written so you can hand it over as it is. The first paragraph gets the chosen direction's text once you pick.

ONE MORE ORBIT: key art brief

The game. A one-button rhythm game. A small flame-spirit, Eko, rides rings round planets in time with the music to bring their songs home from the Hush, a vast silence that has taken them. Mood: mature, mysterious, hand-made. No cute faces; character comes from silhouette, light and movement.

Direction. [the chosen direction's paragraph: medium, palette rule, how light works, with the four stills attached as reference for mood only, not for copying]

Files

  • Layered source (PSD or Procreate) and every layer exported as PNG with transparency, sRGB.
  • Glows and light on their own layers, never baked into the art (the game adds them).
  • One light direction throughout: on the map, from the lighthouse at the centre; on characters, from Eko.
  • Texture fine enough that paper grain is about one pixel at the sizes above.

Rules

  • Fully original. Nothing that resembles an existing game's characters, and no generated imagery in the source files.
  • Full rights assigned to the project; credit in the game.
  • Three rounds: silhouette sketches (three options per character), a colour key, finals.

How the files go into the game

  • They live in the project under public/art/, packed to WebP with a PNG fallback, about 6 MB in total.
  • The game's canvas draws each layer as a sprite and moves the layers itself: the wisp follows on a spring, the cloth sways, the lid opens with the boss's phases, backdrops slide at different speeds as the camera moves.
  • Menus and story cards use the same files, so map, cards and play are one world. Rings, marks and hazards stay drawn by the game, on top, for clarity.
